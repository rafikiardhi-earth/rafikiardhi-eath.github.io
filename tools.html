Tools for researchers

Free tools treat the soil horizon by horizon. Our advanced methods treat it as one connected system, continuous with depth and across the landscape, because that is how water, carbon and energy actually move.

Horizon sums can overestimate stocks

Summing horizons assumes each layer is uniform. Our published work shows this can substantially overestimate carbon stocks compared with assessing the profile at every point. For baselines, credits or reporting, we calculate stocks from depth-continuous profiles with uncertainty.

Sampling for a map: using the variogram

The calculator above sizes a sample to estimate one average. For a map, what matters is how far apart samples are compared with the variogram range. This finds how many samples on a regular grid give a chosen kriging error, from a variogram you already have (for example from the variogram tool or a nearby study).

Map error is the root mean kriging error at unsampled locations, as a share of the standard deviation of the property. 100% means no better than the mean; the nugget sets the lowest error any density can reach.

Check a result: yours or a published one

Use it before you submit, or when reviewing. Kriging accuracy is set by the variogram and by how far apart the samples are, so a reported error can be checked without the data. This lays out samples at the stated density, works out the kriging variance at each one from the stated variogram, and gives the cross-validation RMSE you should expect if that variogram is right.

If the nugget isn't reported, leave it at 0: that is the most generous assumption, so a result that still looks too good really is.

Kriging variance from the variogram and sample layout alone: Burgess, T.M., Webster, R., McBratney, A.B. (1981). J. Soil Sci. 32, 643–659. doi:10.1111/j.1365-2389.1981.tb01737.x Webster, R., Oliver, M.A. (2007). Geostatistics for Environmental Scientists, 2nd ed. Wiley.

Reading the results

  • Nugget : sill is the share of variation you can't predict at your sampling scale: measurement error plus variation over distances shorter than your closest samples. Under 25% is strong spatial dependence, 25–75% moderate, over 75% weak.
  • Range is the distance beyond which samples stop telling you about each other. If your samples are mostly further apart than the range, kriging can do little better than the mean, however good the software.
  • RMSE vs. the mean is the real test: it shows how much spatial prediction actually adds. A map made by kriging is only worth its colours if this is clearly above zero.
  • MSDR and interval coverage show whether the uncertainty is honest. MSDR near 1 and coverage near 90% mean the kriging variance can be trusted; otherwise the uncertainty map is too optimistic or too cautious.
  • Two limits that are rarely stated. Cross-validated RMSE can't fall much below the square root of the nugget, because that part of the variation isn't predictable from neighbours. And when samples are further apart than the range, RMSE approaches the standard deviation of the data. A published RMSE well below these limits usually means the validation points weren't independent of the calibration data, the variogram came from a different dataset, or the error was reported on a different scale.

Reading the indices

Richness counts classes. Shannon (H′) rises with both more classes and more even shares. Evenness (J′) is Shannon relative to its maximum for that many classes, from 0 (one class dominates) to 1 (all equal). Simpson is the chance that two random points fall in different classes.

Hill numbers give the "effective number of classes": N1 weights classes by their share, N2 counts only the common ones. Dominance is the share of the most common class.

Diversity is more than counting classes

Taxonomic indices treat every class as equally different and ignore where soils sit and how they change with depth. Our approach measures pedodiversity from continuous soil properties in three dimensions, as the organisation of energy across the soil system.
