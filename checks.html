Check a result

Plausibility checks for soil and environmental results: kriging error, R² vs RMSE, classification accuracy, detectable change, back-transformation, carbon stock arithmetic, area estimation, effective sample size, spatial cross-validation, lab conversions and coordinates.

Before you submit, or when you review, check that the numbers can all be true. Each check takes the figures a paper or report gives (or your own) and tests them against what the method allows. Nothing you enter leaves your browser.

Finding the right pixel size

Grid resolution is often chosen by habit (30 m because that’s Landsat). Hengl (2006) set out rules that tie it to what the data can actually support. For each aspect of a dataset they give a coarsest legible resolution (the largest pixel that still represents it), a finest legible resolution (below which pixels “are probably just waste of memory”) and a recommended compromise. Fill in whatever you know; the overall range is where the rules agree.

Reference

Hengl, T. (2006). Finding the right pixel size. Computers & Geosciences 32, 1283–1298. doi:10.1016/j.cageo.2005.11.008

Back-transformation

A mean calculated on log values, then exponentiated, is the median (geometric mean), not the mean. For skewed soil properties the difference is often 10–30%. Enter what the paper gives on the log scale.

References

Aitchison, J., Brown, J.A.C. (1957). The Lognormal Distribution. Cambridge University Press.

Journel, A.G. (1980). The lognormal approach to predicting local distributions of selective mining unit grades. Mathematical Geology 12, 285–303. doi:10.1007/BF01029417

Webster, R., Oliver, M.A. (2007). Geostatistics for Environmental Scientists, 2nd ed. Wiley.

Carbon stock arithmetic

Rebuild a reported stock from its inputs. If it doesn’t match, the check tries the common slips (% vs g/kg, stones ignored or on the wrong basis, organic matter instead of carbon, kg/m² vs Mg/ha) and tells you which one reproduces the reported number.

References

Poeplau, C., Vos, C., Don, A. (2017). Soil organic carbon stocks are systematically overestimated by misuse of the parameters bulk density and rock fragment content. SOIL 3, 61–66. doi:10.5194/soil-3-61-2017

Pribyl, D.W. (2010). A critical review of the conventional SOC to SOM conversion factor. Geoderma 156, 75–83. doi:10.1016/j.geoderma.2010.02.003

Area from a confusion matrix

Counting pixels in a map gives biased class areas, because every map has errors. With a stratified accuracy sample, the confusion matrix gives unbiased areas with confidence intervals (Olofsson et al. 2014). Rows are map classes, columns are reference classes, and the last column is each map class’s mapped area.

Reference

Olofsson, P., Foody, G.M., Herold, M., Stehman, S.V., Woodcock, C.E., Wulder, M.A. (2014). Good practices for estimating area and assessing accuracy of land change. Remote Sensing of Environment 148, 42–57. doi:10.1016/j.rse.2014.02.015

Effective sample size

Samples close together carry overlapping information. For estimating an average, a cluster of ten nearby samples can be worth little more than one. This uses the variogram to work out how many independent samples your layout is really worth.

References

Cressie, N.A.C. (1993). Statistics for Spatial Data, revised ed. Wiley. Section 1.3.

Webster, R., Oliver, M.A. (2007). Geostatistics for Environmental Scientists, 2nd ed. Wiley.

Spatial cross-validation and leakage

Random cross-validation on spatial data usually tests points close to the training data, so it reports accuracy the map won’t achieve between samples. Tick anything that applies to the study you’re checking.

References

Roberts, D.R. et al. (2017). Cross-validation strategies for data with temporal, spatial, hierarchical, or phylogenetic structure. Ecography 40, 913–929. doi:10.1111/ecog.02881

Meyer, H., Pebesma, E. (2021). Predicting into unknown space? Estimating the area of applicability of spatial prediction models. Methods in Ecology and Evolution 12, 1620–1633. doi:10.1111/2041-210X.13650

Milà, C., Mateu, J., Pebesma, E., Meyer, H. (2022). Nearest neighbour distance matching leave-one-out cross-validation for map validation. Methods in Ecology and Evolution 13, 1304–1316. doi:10.1111/2041-210X.13851

Coordinates and distances

A degree of longitude shrinks with latitude, so distances calculated directly on longitude and latitude are distorted, and “metres” computed from degrees are wrong. Convert a distance, or paste coordinates to check them.

Method

No paper needed: standard spherical geometry (haversine formula, mean Earth radius 6,371 km).
